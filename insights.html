01 / MACROECONOMICS · ANALYTICAL NOTE

Why interest rates reach far beyond financial markets

Monetary policy shapes financing costs, demand and investment decisions. For import-dependent businesses, exchange-rate developments and funding conditions can interact, but the size and direction of those effects depend on local institutions and market conditions.

Read the analysis ↗

A change in interest rates may influence corporate borrowing costs and household spending, while expectations and financial conditions can transmit policy choices through exchange rates and investment. Businesses should track both direct funding expenses and downstream demand, rather than treating any single policy change as a guaranteed directional signal.

Useful questions: How exposed are costs to local currency moves? What is the financing horizon? Is the demand response immediate or delayed?

02 / GLOBAL TRADE · ANALYTICAL NOTE

Trade policy is also a supply-chain decision

Tariffs and import rules can change the delivered cost of goods and influence sourcing, production locations and compliance requirements.

Read the analysis ↗

When a tariff changes, the effect extends beyond the invoice price. Importers may need to revisit sourcing, Incoterms, shipment timing, documentation, contract terms and customs classifications. The outcome depends on pass-through, competition and the ability of suppliers to adapt.

Good strategy separates short-term cost management from long-term supply-chain resilience.

03 / AUTOMOTIVE ECONOMICS · ANALYTICAL NOTE

What really drives the landed cost of an imported car?

Vehicle pricing is a cross-functional calculation involving acquisition costs, freight, insurance, duties, tax treatment, currency exposure and regulation.

Read the analysis ↗

Even when a manufacturer’s base price stays unchanged, the final landed cost may move as foreign exchange, shipping arrangements or customs obligations change. Timing also matters: regulatory approvals, documentation and delivery can affect working capital and speed to market.

Comparing alternative sourcing paths requires a consistent cost model and clearly documented assumptions.
